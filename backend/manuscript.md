1.1. Introduction  
Overview of African Swine Fever (ASF) as a highly contagious and devastating disease in swine populations.  
---THERE MAY BE ERRORS WITH THE FOLLOWING TEXT---
BEGIN  
African Swine Fever (ASF) is a highly contagious viral disease that affects both domestic and wild pigs, posing a significant threat to the global swine industry and causing severe economic losses [1]. With a mortality rate of up to 100% in affected populations, ASF is considered one of the most devastating diseases in pig farming, leading to widespread depopulation and trade restrictions [2]. The ASF virus is notably resilient, capable of surviving in various environments, including contaminated surfaces and meat products, which facilitates its rapid transmission across regions [3]. Despite its severe impact, there is currently no effective vaccine or treatment available for ASF, underscoring the critical importance of stringent prevention and control measures to mitigate outbreaks and limit its spread [4].

Economic and agricultural impact of ASF outbreaks globally.  
---THERE MAY BE ERRORS WITH THE FOLLOWING TEXT---
BEGIN  
African Swine Fever (ASF) outbreaks have had profound economic and agricultural impacts globally, with estimated losses reaching $130 billion between 2018 and 2020 [5]. China, the world’s largest pork producer, experienced a devastating loss of over 40% of its pig population in 2019 due to ASF, which triggered a sharp increase in pork prices [6]. Similarly, Vietnam culled over 6 million pigs in 2019, representing 20% of its pig population, severely affecting local farmers and the agricultural sector [5]. In the European Union, efforts to combat ASF between 2014 and 2020 cost over €1.3 billion, including compensation for farmers and control measures [7]. Furthermore, ASF outbreaks disrupted the global pork trade, with countries like Germany and Poland facing export bans and reduced market access, exacerbating economic challenges [8]. These examples underscore the widespread and far-reaching consequences of ASF on both local and global scales.

Brief history of ASF vaccine development efforts.  

The development of an effective vaccine for African Swine Fever (ASF) has been a challenging endeavor spanning several decades. Initial efforts in the 1960s focused on inactivated viruses, but these attempts were largely unsuccessful due to the virus's complex nature and inability to induce robust immunity [9]. Subsequently, live attenuated vaccines emerged as a promising alternative, demonstrating some efficacy in experimental settings; however, safety concerns, including the risk of reversion to virulence, have hindered their widespread application [10]. In recent years, advancements in molecular biology have revolutionized vaccine development, leading to the exploration of safer and more targeted approaches, such as subunit and mRNA vaccines [11]. Despite these innovations, a significant obstacle remains the lack of cross-protection between different ASF virus strains, complicating the pursuit of a universal vaccine [12]. These challenges underscore the need for continued research and innovation in ASF vaccine development.

1.2. Current Vaccine Approaches  
Description of live attenuated vaccines and their limitations (e.g., safety concerns, incomplete protection).  
---THERE MAY BE ERRORS WITH THE FOLLOWING TEXT---
BEGIN  
Live attenuated vaccines for African Swine Fever (ASF) are developed by weakening the virus to induce immunity without causing disease, but they present significant safety concerns, including the risk of reversion to virulence [19]. Additionally, these vaccines often provide incomplete protection, as they may fail to prevent infection or transmission in all vaccinated animals, undermining their efficacy [20]. The development of live attenuated ASF vaccines is further complicated by the virus's complex genome and the absence of a robust cell culture system for effective attenuation [21]. Moreover, vaccinated animals may experience adverse reactions, such as fever and tissue damage, which limit the widespread application of these vaccines [12]. These limitations highlight the challenges in utilizing live attenuated vaccines as a reliable solution for ASF control.

Inactivated vaccines for African Swine Fever (ASF) have demonstrated limited efficacy, primarily due to the virus's complex structure and its ability to evade host immune responses [22]. Subunit vaccines and their challenges in eliciting robust immune responses.  
---THERE MAY BE ERRORS WITH THE FOLLOWING TEXT---
BEGIN  
Subunit vaccines for African Swine Fever (ASF) represent a promising yet challenging approach in vaccine development. These vaccines, which rely on a limited set of antigens, often struggle to elicit robust immune responses, as they fail to provide the broad immunological stimulation required for comprehensive protection [26]. The absence of effective adjuvants further compounds this issue, diminishing their ability to stimulate strong and durable immune reactions [27]. Additionally, subunit vaccines face inherent difficulties in mimicking the natural infection process, which is critical for inducing a well-rounded immune response [28]. Variability in immune responses among different pig populations further complicates their efficacy, highlighting the need for tailored solutions in ASF vaccine development [29]. Moreover, the requirement for multiple booster doses to achieve even partial protection renders subunit vaccines impractical for large-scale vaccination programs, underscoring the need for innovative strategies to enhance their immunogenicity and practicality [30].

Viral vector-based vaccines and their progress in ASF research.  
---THERE MAY BE ERRORS WITH THE FOLLOWING TEXT---
BEGIN  
Viral vector-based vaccines have emerged as a promising strategy in African Swine Fever (ASF) research, leveraging vectors such as adenovirus and poxvirus to elicit immune responses against the African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV). Preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of these platforms, with recombinant adenovirus expressing ASFV antigens showing partial protection in pigs [31][32]. Additionally, poxvirus-based vectors, including modified vaccinia Ankara (MVA), have been explored for their ability to induce robust immune responses, further advancing ASFV vaccine development [33]. However, challenges remain, including the need to ensure long-term immunity and address safety concerns related to the immunogenicity of the viral vectors [25]. Despite these hurdles, viral vector-based vaccines continue to be a key focus in the ongoing quest for an effective ASFV vaccine.
